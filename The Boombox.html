Worst to Best: Every Prince Album Ranked

Ranking Prince's dozens of studio albums from worst to best presents quite an interesting series of challenges. After all, the Minneapolis-born genius not only re-invented his own sound dramatically over the years, he helped change the course of pop music itself several times during his illustrious career.

How does one compare the bracingly raw sex tales of his 1980 triumph Dirty Mind to the genre-busting "rock meets punk funk" sound of Purple Rain, or the more lush, sophisticated experimentation of Parade? Well, arguing and voting is how we did it, and in the gallery above you'll find each of Prince's 33 studio efforts analyzed, ranked and ready for your feedback.

We left out live albums, a few jazz-themed instrumental efforts and compilations featuring both his massive collection of hits and newer songs released via his now-dormant internet-based NPG Music Club. So, no Chocolate Invasion, no Slaughterhouse and no Madhouse. But we included everything else from 1979's For You right up to late-era releases like PLECTRUMELECTRUM and Art Official Age. There are also a couple of Prince records disguised as New Power Generation albums. (Come to think of it, we probably could have included the first two albums by the Time, huh?)

So, check out our rankings of the worst and best Prince albums, and let us know how you would have lined them up in the comments section below. Oh and one thing to keep in mind: "worst" is a relative term here. Even the least of these collections feature moments most artists could only dream of achieving, and every single one of them has at least one brilliant song.

35. 'Newpower Soul' (1998)

Although technically credited to his backing New Power Generation band, make no mistake, this is a Prince album – just not a particularly good one. For the most part Newpower Soul is a pleasant but largely forgettable collection of light, poppy funk songs, with the lone exception being the dramatic slow-burner "The One" – which continued to turn up in Prince's set lists for years.

34. 'Planet Earth' (2007)

Prince's mid-'00s comeback hit a bit of a snag on this album, which starts off with two clunkers (the overly didactic environmentally themed title track and the forced levity of "Guitar") and never fully recovers. However, the extended, horn-charged "Chelsea Rogers" is highly recommended.

33. 'The Vault: Old Friends 4 Sale' (1999)

As contractually obligated backlog-clearing releases go, this one's ... not bad. These are hardly the first 10 songs any hardcore fan would seek out if allowed entry into Prince's vast collection of officially unreleased tracks, of course. But, the whole thing maintains a pleasingly consistent, breezy jazz-influenced sound, best evidenced on the eight-minute long "She Spoke 2 Me."

32. 'Rave Un2 the Joy Fantastic' (1999)

Here's the album that proves Prince is physically incapable of playing it safe, even when he tries. This was the plan: Sign with noted hit-maker Clive Davis' Arista Records, then line up all-star collaborators such as Sheryl Crow and Gwen Stefani in hopes of replicating Santana's career-rejuvenating Supernatural album. Unfortunately, our hero is simply too unique and idiosyncratic to fit into the modern hit-making machine. So while there were several highlights, overall Rave was too uneven and all over the map to accomplish its commercial or creative goals.

31. 'PLECTRUMELECTRUM' (2014)

Ever since he turned his incredible guitar skills loose for the first time on 1979's "Bambi," a certain segment of Prince's fan base has been dying for him to make a full-on rock album. When he began touring with the three-piece, keyboard-less 3rdyegirl lineup, it seemed we might finally get his Band of Gypsys-like Jimi Hendrix moment. But PLETCRUMELECTRUM once again finds Prince too restless to keep from blending genres for any extended length of time. So the record instead simply features a more guitar-reliant mix of his usual rock, funk and pop styles, weighed down at times by uncharacteristically corny lyrics.

30. 'HITnRun Phase One' (2015)

Less than 12 months after ending an unprecedented four-year break from the record shelves with two different albums, Prince seemed to have made a full return to his typically frantic pace with the release of this admirably experimental album. Like a wily cat playing with a new ball of yarn, he incorporates EDM and other modern pop and hip-hop influences into his work fairly effortlessly.

29. 'Chaos and Disorder' (1996)

This is another of the contractually mandated albums Prince delivered to free himself from his Warner Bros. Records contract, and once again it's a surprising (if minor) triumph that benefits from its modest aspirations. The liner notes state that Chaos and Disorder was "originally intended 4 private use only," and it feels like you've been invited into a late-night recording session featuring one of the best NPG lineups of all time. Standout tracks include the chunky, funky guitar workout "I Like it There" and the swaggering "I Rock, Therefore I Am."

28. 'MPLSound' (2009)

Don't get us wrong, it's a blast hearing Prince re-visit the electronic funk of his early '80s heyday. And there's absolutely nothing wrong with the light-hearted, low-stakes subject matter featured in most of these songs. It all simply doesn't stick in the mind or heart as much as the true works of genius we're going to start talking about real soon.

27. 'The Rainbow Children' (2001)

This might be Prince's least-heralded album ever – and no doubt a good many people were turned off by the heavy-handed religious subject matter, which celebrated his recent conversion to the Jehovah's Witnesses. But that's a shame, because there's some really sophisticated, envelope-pushing music on The Rainbow Children, particularly the 10-minute-long title track, which pairs catchy "Up with People" vocals straight off the 'Hair' soundtrack with an insanely infectious bass line.

26. 'Lotusflow3r' (2009)

This is the more dynamic, diverse and rock-inclusive third of a package that also included the aforementioned MPLSound as well as the debut album from Prince protege Bria Valente. Prince's guitar is in fine form on the instrumental showcase "From the Lotus ..." and the downright Hendrix-ian "Dreamer," but the real highlight is the impossibly peppy, genre-defying retro-rocker named "$."

25. 'Exodus' (1995)

Once again operating under the NPG banner – this time wearing a mask and performing under the name "Tora Tora" as a result of his bitter battle to remove himself from Warner Bros. – Prince unleashes some of the deepest, darkest George Clinton-influenced funk of his career. If you haven't heard them already, you simply must seek out "Return of the Bump Squad" and the epic Parliament-inspired title track right this moment.

24. '20Ten' (2010)

While no new ground is broken here, there are some works of "typical" Prince genius that are well worth your time. The yearning, outer-space funk of "Beginning Endlessly" is one of his best songs of the past two decades, as is the effortlessly upbeat "Lavaux" and the self-depreciating "Laydown," which finds Prince awesomely referring to himself as "the Purple Yoda" from the heart of Minnesota.

23. 'Come' (1994)

This is the first of the three albums Prince released partially or fully against his will in order to extract himself from a record label contract he found creatively restrictive and financially unfair. It's also the best: full of bold, successful experiments such as the slinky, simmering title track, the drop dead gorgeous bass-heavy "Space" and the disturbing child abuse tale "Papa."

22. '3121' (2006)

After successfully re-capturing the public's attention with the more eager-to-please Musicology, Prince got a little more daring and a bit weirder with this follow-up. The hard-hitting and gloriously off-kilter beat of the title track is worth the purchase price alone – but wait there's more! You also get the spare, seductive dance floor burner "Black Sweat" and the lovely Latin-tinged ballad "Te Amo Corazon."

21. 'Art Official Age' (2014)

Clearly the stronger of the two albums released in 2014, this largely solo effort finds Prince updating his typical bag of tricks in some cool new ways while exploring a loose sci-fi theme about time travel or cryogenics. Most importantly, it's his best batch of fully-realized songs in over a decade, with at least four all-time mixtape-worthy classics: "Clouds," "You Know," "Breakfast Can Wait" and "This Could Be Us."

20. 'Crystal Ball / The Truth' (1998)

As with the aforementioned The Vault: Old Friends 4 Sale, this three-disc collection is too focused on the mid-'90s to be the true treasure chest we all know Prince has hiding at Paisley Park. But there's more than enough gold to be found here, including the long-awaited release of the Sign o' the Times-era title track, the throbbing "What's My Name" and the absolutely hilarious Morris Day drum lesson "Cloreen Baconskin." As a bonus, this album also came with The Truth, a completely acoustic album of largely interesting new songs.

19. 'Musicology' (2004)

After dedicating much of the previous five years to releasing a dizzying array of (not always essential) music via the internet or his own direct-to-fans imprint, Prince returned to a major label with his most crowd-pleasing collection of songs in years. With Musicology, Prince quickly won his way back into the spotlight. Head straight to the warm, James Brown-inspired title track, which celebrated the virtues of "real" musicianship over the computer-assisted productions dominating the pop landscape.

18. 'Emancipation' (1996)

Finally free from his nasty record label battle, Prince celebrated as only he can or would – by releasing a three-album, (exactly) three-hour long collection of songs that showed off the full range of his expansive writing, performing and vocal talents. In the best way possible, there's entirely too much greatness to digest here, but that doesn't mean you shouldn't spend a lot of time trying.

17. 'Batman' Soundtrack (1989)

Originally tasked with writing a couple of tracks for the first of the modern-day superhero blockbusters, Prince naturally went overboard, delivering an entire album filled with songs told from the point of view of Batman, the Joker and Bruce Wayne. Although the silly pastiche of "Batdance" was the big hit, the true works of art here are the insistent, pulsing "The Future" and the Stevie Wonder throwback "Vicki Waiting."

16. 'Graffiti Bridge' (1990)

Although neither the film nor the album versions of the sequel to Purple Rain made anywhere near the impact of the originals, there's a wide range of highly inventive and enjoyable material to be had here. The best of the bunch might just be the future blues of "The Question of U," although the soaring gospel of "Still Will Stand All Time" is also hard to ignore.

15. ‘HITnRUN Phase Two’ (2015)

Just three months after the more experimental ‘Phase One,’ Prince surprise-released its much more traditional twin. Partially composed of re-revised versions of songs the Purple one had leaked online or performed live over the years, this horn-heavy album turned out to be the most cohesive, honest-to-goodness album he’d released in at least 20 years. Unbelievably, it would also turn out to be the last released in his lifetime.

14. 'For You' (1978)

The relatively simplistic R&B grooves of his debut album offered only the slightest hint of the incredible range of musical styles Prince would incorporate into his music over his impressive career. But For You showed that this astonishingly talented one-man-band already knew his way around a hook with catchy songs such as "In Love" and "Soft and Wet."

13. 'The Gold Experience' (1995)

Although his music has always trumpeted the virtues of peace and love, there's no denying that being supremely pissed off at his record label lit a fire under Prince's butt while he was composing the songs for this album. The result was his most consistent, confident and daring collection of songs since the 1987 masterpiece Sign o' the Times, with the hard-hitting "P Control," the gritty gang-warfare tale "Shy" and the dramatic album-closing "Gold" all worthy of special attention.

12. 'Love Symbol Album' (1992)

Clearly energized by his amazing New Power Generation band, Prince quickly followed up on the multi-platinum triumph of 1991's Diamonds and Pearls with this futuristic rock opera. The hilariously profane retro-soul of "Sexy MF," the sultry reggae of "Blue Light" and the stunningly original, Indian-influenced "7" are all standout tracks, but the most impressive thing is how well all these disparate styles flow together into one unified album.

11. 'Diamonds and Pearls' (1991)

Apparently deciding that too many people were following his old working methods, Prince ditched the synthesizers and drum machines in favor of organic instrumentation for his 13th studio album. Assembling his second-best ever band (we're staying loyal to the crack late '80s outfit featured on 'Lovesexy' and the Black Album), the Purple One then turned them loose on a typically fantastic set of songs, including the booming "Gett Off," the T-Rex-inspired "Cream" and the ruminative "Money Don't Matter 2 Night."

10. 'Lovesexy' (1988)

After suddenly deciding not to release the somewhat raunchy Black Album as the follow-up to his career landmark Sign o' the Times, Prince quickly assembled this much more positive and inspirational collection of songs. While he's still spending part of his time looking for romantic trysts in his daddy's Thunderbird, by far the greater focus of songs like "Anna Stesia," "I WIsh U Heaven" and "Positivity" is on spiritual fulfillment. (Here's a tip: Find and buy all the 12" singles from this era, especially "Glam Slam.")

9. 'Prince' (1979)

Although it still sounds sonically tame compared to the legendary run of records that would start just one year later, Prince's self-titled second album represented a bold step forward from his debut. This is home to the original version of Chaka Khan's big hit "I Feel for You," the early-career calling card "I Wanna Be Your Lover" and the explosive guitar-charged rocker "Bambi."

8. 'Around the World in a Day' (1985)

Perhaps the most impressive thing about this album is how far Prince ran from the star-making sound of its predecessor, Purple Rain. While some growing pains are on display at times here, psychedelic-hued triumphs such as "Raspberry Beret" and "Pop Life" proved that their creator would never need to rely on repeating past triumphs.

7. 'The Black Album' (1987 / 1994)

There's a popular school of thought which suggests this storming tour de force has been overrated as a result of the mystique surrounding its original bootleg-only availability. Nonsense. With his best-ever band still white-hot from the Sign o' the Times tour, Prince delivered a tightly-focused collection of deep funk and R&B and watched them hit it far, far out of the park. If you can sit still through this entire record, you have our respect but not our admiration.

6. 'Controversy' (1981)

Compared to the rest of his classic '80s albums, Controversy didn't present any big new advances in Prince's sound, instead "merely" expanding and brightening the territory first mined on 1980's Dirty Mind. But when the results are as inspired as the title track, "Sexuality" and "Let's Work," well ... seriously, who gives a damn?

5. 'Parade' (1986)

It may not have shifted the music world off its axis like Purple Rain, and it doesn't feature the mind-blowing range of Sign o' the Times. But for 45 minutes, Parade takes you to a wonderful alternate universe filled with exciting musical possibilities, fulfilling the promise of Prince's genius in a way most of his albums only hint at. Also important to note: while the tightly wound "Kiss" gets all the attention, the soaring "Mountains" might actually be the better song.

4. 'Dirty Mind' (1980)

After two albums of polite, polished pop songs about hugs and kisses, Prince finally lets his freak flag fly on this down and dirty collection of lyrically frank songs about sex, sex and more sex. Of course, all this shocking lyrical content wouldn't matter a bit if the music behind it wasn't an equally bracingly raw blend of funk, new wave and dance music.

3. '1999' (1982)

1999 is the album where Prince perfected the synthesizer and drum machine-heavy "Minneapolis Sound" that would define much of popular music for the next decade or so: No big deal, right? Releasing a double album while still merely on the cusp of worldwide fame was a typically risky move, and it paid off big time with hits such as the anti-war title track, the sultry "Little Red Corvette" and the updated rockabilly of "Delirious."

2. 'Sign o' the Times' (1987)

Sign o' the Times isn't a masterpiece simply because it visits and blends so many different styles of music in more assured, unexpected ways than Prince had ever accomplished before. Instead, what makes the album so special is how connected to the real world the whole thing feels. If there's one overall critique you can hang on Prince's recorded music, it's that the songs often seem to emanate from a reality removed from our own – a slightly synthetic, idealized place in the future or outer space or something. But there are grains and textures and earth tones all over Sign o' the Times and the heartbreaks, struggles and salvation he describes on its 16 amazing songs all carry much more weight as a result.

1. 'Purple Rain' (1984)

If 1999 was the album where Prince's music went widescreen, then Purple Rain is where everything exploded into 3-D. It trades the extended dance grooves of his previous triumph for a much sharper, tighter collection of songs that fearlessly defy pop conventions, then adds an extra-heavy dose of rock guitar to almost every one of them, creating a sound nobody else has come close to re-creating yet.
